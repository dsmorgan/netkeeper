The triage screen (`/triage`, spec 10.2): one contact at a time with its evidence, the keyboard map, progress, the skipped-queue filter, and the bulk suggestion banner. `m` met, `n` not met, `s` skip, `u` undo, `t` tag, `p` edit the preferred name, `→` next without deciding, and `?` for a help overlay that lists all of them; the keys are handled on the window, so they work from a cold page with focus nowhere in particular, and they stand aside for a modifier, for an already-handled event, for anything typed into a field, and for an auto-repeat, because a judgement about a person is not something you hold a key down for. The `p` editor and the `t` picker belong to the contact they were opened for and close themselves when the card moves on, so a name typed for one person can never be submitted against the next. A write that does not land names its contact in a banner that the next keystroke does not erase and that `u` does not offer to take back — and an undo pressed underneath a decision still in flight is dropped rather than reaching past it to the one before, which would otherwise revert a contact silently, since both sit behind the frontier. Two cards are held at a time and the decision *is* the fetch — `POST /triage/decisions` returns the card after the one now on screen — so a run costs one request per contact and a keystroke never waits for the network; `→` moves on with a `GET` that writes nothing, tracking the furthest contact the server has handed over rather than deriving it from the buffer, so a mix of `m` and `→` never doubles back over somebody already passed. Undo's `409` is put to the person as a choice, worded separately for each of its three reasons — a field changed since the decision, the contact was archived, or it was merged into another — with the backend's own account of what diverged and `force` offered, never taken; a restored contact this queue would not serve — one whose state is out of the filter, or one that a force overrode an archive or a merge to recover — is reported rather than rendered as the next card, while a force over an ordinary edited field keeps the card it was already showing, and because tagging goes through the contacts API and not the triage log, the screen names what `u` will actually take back and says so when a tag has happened since. The bulk banner previews with a count, sends that count back with the apply, and treats a `409` as a preview to take again rather than an error. Message bodies, timeline summaries, and notes are rendered as text through an inert `DOMParser` transform that reads through a body the archive cut mid-tag (issue #75); `dangerouslySetInnerHTML` appears nowhere on the screen. The card is a polite atomic live region that never moves focus, so a screen reader announces each contact while the keyboard map stays live, and the evidence panel is its own landmark beside it — including the company overlap, labelled as overlap with the rest of the address book rather than a shared history the schema has no way to know, with the companies nobody else is at left out. Fifty contacts were driven through the keyboard in 210 ms against a real backend, 4.2 ms each, at exactly one request per contact; the same run against the offline fake in `frontend/src/features/triage/triage-throughput.test.tsx` takes about 750 ms, because its deliberate 15 ms delay dominates. The claim that matters is the one the fake does pin: at any pace slower than a round trip every card change is synchronous, asserted with nothing awaited between the keystroke and reading the card.
